\begin{abstract}
Interactive narrative systems increasingly combine language generation, state tracking, and scene-image synthesis, but this richer output scope can make player-facing latency a central bottleneck. We present \dn, a pre-generation based interactive narrative system designed for \emph{full-ready} multimodal delivery, where the next turn is counted as ready only when both the textual continuation and the corresponding scene image are available. On a 20-item formal benchmark, a simulated 60-second player reading interval reduces \dn's mean next-turn full-ready latency from 27.095 seconds to 5.299 seconds and its median latency from 24.571 seconds to 0.027 seconds, while preserving a strict success rate of 1.000. We further summarize released evidence that \dn outperforms three image-generation baselines on judged visual quality, exceeds the current DOC fallback baseline on judged text coherence, and exhibits mechanism-level ablation patterns consistent with the proposed pre-generation design. Together, these results suggest that reading-time-aware pre-generation can make full-ready multimodal narrative delivery substantially more practical for interactive use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interactive narrative systems must balance rich multimodal generation with low user-perceived latency. Unlike single-turn text or image generators, a playable narrative system must maintain story state, expose actionable options, advance the plot after a user decision, and deliver the next piece of content in a form that the player can immediately continue from. When the next turn contains both text and a scene image, the relevant efficiency question is therefore not merely how quickly a model can produce an isolated image, but how quickly the system can make the complete next turn usable.

\dn addresses this problem through a pre-generation based delivery mechanism. Instead of waiting until the user clicks the next action to begin all downstream work, \dn uses the player's reading interval to prepare likely next-turn content in the background. This design turns reading time into computation time and aims to reduce the latency that remains visible to the player. The present draft evaluates this mechanism across efficiency, visual quality, narrative/text quality, and component-level ablations, so that the paper can jointly address whether \dn is fast enough for interaction and whether its delivered content is strong enough for narrative play.

The current efficiency results support three stage-level findings. First, strict full-ready delivery without dwell time remains expensive because the system must wait for both textual and visual readiness. Second, a realistic reading interval substantially changes the latency distribution: in the pregen60 setting, most samples are delivered nearly immediately after the next click. Third, comparisons against image-continuation references should be interpreted as scope-aware references rather than fully isomorphic system comparisons, because \dn returns text plus image plus playable state while the baselines report image or workflow latency.

\section{System Overview}
\dn is an interactive narrative generation system that combines structured story-state management, option generation, and scene-image delivery. At each turn, the system presents narrative text and choices to the player, receives the selected action, updates the story state, and prepares the next multimodal scene. The paper's central efficiency hypothesis is that pre-generation can reduce user-visible delay by using the idle interval between content delivery and the player's next action.

The pre-generation mechanism maintains candidate next-turn work in the background. During the player's reading interval, \dn prepares likely continuations and their associated scene images. When the player later selects an action, the system attempts to serve a strict full-ready result from the prepared content; if the requested content is not yet fully available, the measured path waits until both text and image are ready. This distinction is important for the evaluation: a response is not counted as successful merely because a placeholder or partial output exists.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate efficiency on a 20-item formal benchmark subset derived from the DN-quality benchmark. Each item defines a narrative theme and constraints that can be mapped either to \dn's full interactive pipeline or to image-generation baseline adapters. The evaluation uses the latest v15 composite20 artifacts as the source of truth and excludes earlier v14, fixed-v2, and trimmed-sample exploratory results from the main paper tables.

The primary comparison is not intended to claim that all systems are architecturally identical. Instead, it measures the latency of \dn's full-ready delivery and reports image-baseline latencies as reference points for the image-continuation subtask. This scope-aware framing is necessary because \dn performs story-state update and textual continuation in addition to scene-image delivery, whereas StoryDiffusion, SDM-v2, and IC-LoRA are evaluated as next-turn image or image-workflow references.

\section{Conclusion}
The current paper draft now supports a coherent scope-aware conclusion across efficiency, visual quality, and text-side quality. First, the efficiency results show that strict full-ready next-turn delivery remains expensive when no dwell time is available, but that pre-generation substantially changes the user-visible latency profile under a realistic reading interval. On the latest v15 composite20 protocol, \dn reduces mean full-ready latency from 27.095 seconds in the zero-dwell normal20 setting to 5.299 seconds in the pregen60 setting, while preserving a strict success rate of 1.000. This result establishes pre-generation as a practically meaningful systems mechanism rather than a cosmetic optimization.

Second, the quality sections indicate that faster delivery is not purchased at the cost of unusable outputs. On the released judged image-comparison summary, \dn scores above StoryDiffusion, SDM-v2, and IC-LoRA, while its internal CLIP-I and DINOv2 image-pair proxies show moderate but non-trivial protagonist--scene continuity. On the text side, \dn also performs strongly on the currently executable judged subset against the DOC fallback baseline, and the aligned GenAgents notes show that the remaining trade-off is primarily one of system orientation---structured planning and full-system stability versus lighter-weight persona-conditioned response---rather than simple coherence failure.

Third, the available mechanism analyses suggest that the present configuration of \dn is not accidental. The main-ready evidence shows that reading-time-aware pre-generation materially improves full-ready delivery, while earlier controlled studies further suggest that pre-generation, council planning, and read-wait strategy each affect latency structure, stability, or the probability of returning a real scene instead of a weaker placeholder path. These auxiliary results are not all frozen under the same protocol as the main v15 composite20 table, but they remain useful for interpreting why the full system behaves as it does.

Overall, the current evidence supports the following paper-level claim: \dn is a structured interactive narrative system that can deliver full-ready multimodal next turns with stable success, and its pre-generation mechanism makes this heavier output scope substantially more compatible with interactive use. The remaining work is to further unify the protocol coverage of the auxiliary ablations, enlarge judged quality subsets, and continue reducing long-tail latency and theme-sensitive visual variance.

The appendix tail cases reinforce the main efficiency interpretation. Under \texttt{normal20}, the slowest items are dominated by rows whose cache-miss reason is \texttt{request\_scene\_generation\_in\_progress}, meaning that the second click arrives before the relevant scene generation path has fully completed. Under \texttt{pregen60s20}, most items become near-instant likely hits, and the remaining slow cases are concentrated in a small number of cache-miss or image-not-ready rows such as \texttt{DNQBV1\_018}, \texttt{DNQBV1\_014}, and \texttt{DNQBV1\_009}. This is consistent with the aggregate result that pre-generation sharply improves the center of the latency distribution while leaving a smaller long tail that remains sensitive to scene readiness timing.
